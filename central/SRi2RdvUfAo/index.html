Escamilla Takes the Superspeedway

James Smith and an uncertain second-stage scorer set the table before Juan Escamilla displaced teammate Trevor Haley in the final run.

THE MAIN STORY · 809 WORDS

Juan Escamilla took the high line around teammate Trevor Haley and restored Darkhorse to Victory Lane at iRacing Superspeedway. The pass settled a race built around help: Ethan Moreno’s push carried James Smith to the first supported stage, damaged cars needed partners to recover through the middle, and the final green-white-checkered formation asked Haley and Escamilla to choose between protecting a team position and taking the only position that counted. Escamilla chose the outside route and won, with Haley second and Randy Showers third.

The early checkpoint belonged to Smith, but his Stage 1 result was not a solo act. HLRN’s companion credits Moreno’s push as the force that put Smith in position to collect it. That is the clearest early expression of the race’s drafting economy—one driver’s momentum becoming another driver’s points. It also widened the season’s stage cast after Darkhorse had controlled both Charlotte checkpoints.

By the time the primary broadcast’s strongest surviving sequence begins, the race is already working through a spin and a cycle of damaged cars on pit road. Scott Wise appears in one caution replay, then a later lane movement clips him and carries Aaron Trebig into the wreck. Another late caution arrives without an identifiable cause on the tape. Those interruptions repeatedly break the draft into smaller alliances and make each restart less a continuation than a new attempt to build a viable line.

The late caution periods also produced one of the broadcast’s most distinctly Highline moments when Shawn Stamper’s fan club became part of the break. The feature belongs to the league’s entertainment voice, but the underlying competitive problem remained serious: every yellow reduced the time available for a line to form, and the field would eventually have to decide the race through a green-white-checkered sprint.

Haley led that final formation. He had the preferred immediate position and a teammate among the drivers capable of generating an outside run. Connection trouble complicated the next caution review, but the race returned to the same central question when the field accelerated again: could Haley keep enough help behind him to control both lanes, or would the high side organize before the stripe?

The answer depended on where the drafting help remained attached. Haley could defend one lane from the point, but each block risked weakening the line he left behind. Escamilla did not need to lead the entire sprint; he needed the outside to stay connected long enough for its combined momentum to reach Haley’s quarter panel. Showers remained close enough to benefit from whichever lane advanced. That three-driver geometry gave the finish its stakes without requiring a complete order for the cars stacked behind them.

The green-white-checkered format shortened the time available to repair a bad choice. A driver trapped in the wrong line would not receive a long fuel run or another scheduled stage to recover. Haley’s early control made him the target, Escamilla’s teammate status gave him access to the same organizational help, and Showers’s position kept the podium open beyond Darkhorse. When the lanes began separating, the race became less about protecting a team one-two than deciding which individual car could carry the best run to the stripe.

Escamilla found the answer outside. Haley remained in front through the approach, but the lane behind him weakened and Escamilla’s line carried the stronger final momentum. Randy Showers stayed close enough to the battle to occupy the third supported position. When HLRN reviewed the finish, the No. 99 was placed first, ahead of Haley and Showers. The archive reports the teammate contest as a racing fact, not as evidence of private conflict.

The winner’s interview and repeated finish replays let the broadcast tell the result from more than one angle. Haley also joined the booth, giving the runner-up side of a finish he had led until the last decisive exchange. HLRN’s companion placed Escamilla atop the early standings after four events, but the missing full table means that note remains attributed context rather than a reconstructed points sheet.

iRacing Superspeedway ultimately recorded two forms of collaboration and one act of separation. Moreno helped Smith win a stage; the late field rebuilt itself through shifting draft partners; then Escamilla used the high line to move beyond his own teammate. Darkhorse recovered from Charlotte with an Escamilla–Haley one-two, Showers completed the podium, and the unresolved second stage stayed unresolved. The race is fuller, not weaker, when the uncertainty is kept in its proper place.
