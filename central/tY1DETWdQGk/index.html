THE BEAR ARRIVES

Moreno Takes Over Homestead

Juan Escamilla and Bryce Hinton split the stages before Ethan Moreno turned his Highline debut into a feature win.

Compiled from the HLRN broadcast archive · November 24, 2025

THE MAIN STORY · 909 WORDS

Ethan Moreno did not need an acclimation race. In his first supported HLRN feature appearance, “The Bear” arrived at Homestead-Miami and won, finishing a night that had already passed through Juan Escamilla’s hands in Stage 1 and Bryce Hinton’s in Stage 2. Francisco Bacayo followed Moreno home for a second consecutive runner-up finish, while Escamilla converted his early control into third. The podium confirmed that the league’s second race had expanded the cast without weakening Darkhorse’s hold on the most valuable position.

Bacayo began from the pole with Escamilla beside him and Bowman, Moreno, Craig Rowe, and Matt Brown among the front starters. That grid immediately gave Homestead a different shape from Daytona. Bacayo was no longer simply the driver who had appeared second on the inaugural result board; he was leading the next field to green. The first lap tested the formation almost at once, but the race settled long enough for the broad, side-by-side work associated with Homestead to become the night’s governing problem.

Escamilla solved that problem first. With the field running in lanes at the front, he controlled the opening stage and collected the supported Stage 1 result. Hinton answered later, giving the race a clean exchange of checkpoint authority. The two stage calls matter because neither man ultimately won the feature: Homestead would be decided by a driver who was present in the opening group but had not yet owned either of the race’s formal breaks.

The middle distance became a strategy race. Hinton entered a pit sequence that started moving the order, and tire choices redrew it again. At Homestead, that difference is more than a routine stop; new grip can change which groove is usable and how aggressively a driver can cross the track. The surviving broadcast does not provide a complete running order for every cycle, but it does show that the race Moreno inherited for the finish was not the same race Bacayo had led from the pole.

Evan Perry’s crash preceded a scheduled caution and compressed the field for a ten-lap sprint. Craig Rowe fought toward the top ten before the reset, and three lanes formed when the race returned to the pressure of a short run. Bowman later brought the field back to green, another reminder that the visible lead kept circulating among drivers who were close enough to influence the finish even when they would not occupy the final podium.

With four laps remaining under the next caution order, Moreno controlled the formation. This was the first moment his debut shifted from promising to decisive. Escamilla, already a stage winner, remained near enough to carry the final-lap question. Bacayo had survived the early and middle exchanges, keeping the pole sitter’s route to another podium open. Moreno’s job was no longer to introduce himself to the league; it was to manage a restart against drivers who had already established their strength.

The contenders reached that sprint with different claims on the night. Bacayo had started it from the pole and protected enough track position to remain relevant after the strategy cycles. Escamilla had converted the opening lanes into Stage 1 and still possessed a direct route to the feature. Hinton had taken Stage 2 but no longer controlled the final formation. Moreno had none of those earlier trophies, yet the caution timing placed the race in his hands. Homestead’s last four laps would decide whether early authority or late positioning carried more value.

Moreno’s closing task was therefore more demanding than a clean-air drive to the stripe. Every restart allowed Escamilla to apply the road he had already used to win a stage, while Bacayo could turn his pole speed into a second chance from the draft behind. Moreno kept the launch organized, prevented the lanes from producing a completed move, and preserved the point as the laps disappeared. The tape does not need an invented margin to explain the finish: the important measurement is that the Bear remained first when the remaining opportunities reached zero.

The closing sequence placed Moreno and Escamilla at the center of the final-lap decision. Moreno kept the feature lead and reached the finish as HLRN’s second winner in two official races. The closing readout put Bacayo second and Escamilla third. That order gave the race a satisfying competitive symmetry: the pole sitter retained a place on the podium, the opening-stage winner returned in third, and the driver who had waited through both stage outcomes took the position that ended the night.

Moreno’s post-race segment added personality to the result without changing what the result proved. The companion program could play with rivalries and the larger Darkhorse cast, but the accepted race record remains narrower and stronger: Moreno won, Bacayo finished second, and Escamilla finished third. Bacayo’s second P2 in as many races made him the first repeat podium threat outside the winning team, while Escamilla’s stage-and-podium combination gave him an early two-part result file.

Homestead’s warning to the rest of HLRN was structural. Darkhorse did not need Porter to repeat his Daytona victory because the organization could change closers. Escamilla could win a stage, Hinton could answer in the next one, and Moreno could arrive late in the story to take the feature. Bacayo remained the constant resistance, twice runner-up through two very different races. The complete points table is still missing, but the competitive message survives without it: Highline’s early balance involved many names, while Darkhorse kept finding the final one.
